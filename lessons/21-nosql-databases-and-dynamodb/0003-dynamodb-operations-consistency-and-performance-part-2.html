Picking up immediately from part one — Query, Scan, and capacity units are fresh. This lesson covers how DynamoDB actually replicates data behind the scenes, the two read-consistency models that replication makes possible, and the arithmetic for turning a workload into RCU/WCU settings.

Replication: Leader and Storage Nodes

Every item in a DynamoDB table is replicated across three storage nodes, each in a different Availability Zone. One of the three is dynamically elected the leader node — dynamic meaning that if the leader ever fails, a new election happens automatically.

All writes go to the leader node first. Once the leader has the new data, it's immediately consistent, and replication to the other two nodes begins asynchronously — typically completing within milliseconds, assuming no faults. This is exactly why writes are pricier and less scalable than reads: they're pinned to one specific node, the leader, rather than able to be spread across all three.

Two Read-Consistency Models

Because data briefly exists on only one or two of the three nodes right after a write, DynamoDB offers two ways to read it:

Eventually consistent reads (default)
DynamoDB routes the read to any of the three storage nodes at random. Most of the time all three already agree, so there's no practical difference — but in the brief window right after a write, an eventually consistent read might land on a node that hasn't caught up yet, returning stale data. In exchange for tolerating that small risk, eventually consistent reads cost half the RCU of strongly consistent ones — double the reads per RCU.
Strongly consistent reads
Always routed to the leader node specifically, guaranteeing the most current data. Because it can't load-balance across all three nodes, it's less scalable, and it costs the standard, full RCU rate.
Choosing between the two
Data where staleness is genuinely dangerous — financial balances, inventory counts, medical records — needs strongly consistent reads. Workloads that can tolerate a rare, brief lag in exchange for lower cost and better scaling should default to eventually consistent reads, which is also DynamoDB's out-of-the-box default.

Capacity Planning Arithmetic

Turning a workload into RCU/WCU settings always follows the same two-step pattern: work out the units needed per item, then multiply by operations per second.

Writes: take the average item size, divide by 1 KB (the size of one WCU), and round up. Multiply by writes per second.

Example: 10 writes/second, average item size 2.5 KB. 2.5 ÷ 1 = 2.5, round up to 3 WCU per item. 3 × 10 = 30 WCU required.

Reads: take the average item size, divide by 4 KB (the size of one RCU), and round up. Multiply by reads per second. Halve the result if eventually consistent reads are acceptable.

Example: 10 reads/second, average item size 2.5 KB. 2.5 ÷ 4 rounds up to 1 RCU per item. 1 × 10 = 10 RCU for strongly consistent reads, or 5 RCU if eventually consistent reads will do.

Watch for the per-minute trick
Exam questions sometimes state a rate in per-minute terms — say, "60 writes per minute" — specifically to see whether you'll plug that straight into the formula. RCU and WCU are always per-second values, so any per-minute figure must be converted first (60 per minute becomes 1 per second) before the arithmetic above applies.
