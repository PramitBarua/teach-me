Route 53 Interoperability

Lesson 12 of 13 · ~7 min

Source: Adrian Cantrill SAA-C03 · Amazon Route 53 Concepts

Registering a domain through Route 53 feels like one action, but it's actually performing two separate jobs at once. Pulling those jobs apart makes every "mixed registrar/hosting" scenario much easier to reason about.

Registrar vs. Hosting: Two Different Jobs

  • Domain registrar — accepts your registration fee (per 1 or 3 years) and liaises with the registry for the relevant top-level domain (e.g. the .org registry) to add your domain's entry, pointing at whichever name servers you specify.
  • Domain hosting — allocates name servers and hosts the actual zone file (the public hosted zone) containing your records.

When you register and host with Route 53 in the default way, both jobs happen together: Route 53 creates the hosted zone (hosting), gets its four name servers, and passes them to the registrar side of Route 53, which enters them into the .org registry (registrar). You pay a registration fee (registrar) and a monthly hosted-zone fee (hosting) — conceptually separate charges for conceptually separate services.

Exam tip: "Route 53 as registrar only" is rarely useful
Route 53's registrar function is fairly unremarkable on its own — most of its value is in hosting. Using Route 53 purely as a registrar while a third party hosts the zone gives up most of the benefit. The far more common real-world pattern is the reverse: hosting only — keeping an existing third-party registrar (maybe for an existing business relationship or discount) while pointing that domain's name servers at a Route 53 hosted zone to get Route 53's DNS features.

To set up "hosting only": create a public hosted zone in Route 53 (allocating its four name servers), then update the domain's NS records at the third-party registrar to point at those four Route 53 name servers. This can be done either at initial registration or years later on an already-registered domain.
