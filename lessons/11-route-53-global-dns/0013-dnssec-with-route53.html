Implementing DNSSEC with Route 53

Lesson 13 of 13 · ~8 min

Source: Adrian Cantrill SAA-C03 · Configuring DNSSEC Signing in Amazon Route 53

This is the module's most advanced topic — where cryptographic signing meets the DNS delegation chain covered in Lesson 01. DNSSEC lets a resolver verify that a DNS response genuinely came from the zone's owner and wasn't tampered with in transit.

Two Kinds of Keys

Enabling DNSSEC on a Route 53 hosted zone creates an asymmetric key pair in KMS (in us-east-1 — this is a hard requirement, regardless of which Region your zone otherwise concerns), used to derive the zone's key-signing key (KSK). Route 53 separately creates the zone-signing key (ZSK) entirely internally — KMS is not involved in ZSK creation or management.

KeyBacked byRole
Key-Signing Key (KSK)An asymmetric KMS key you own, in us-east-1Signs the DNSKEY record; its public part is hashed into the parent zone's DS record
Zone-Signing Key (ZSK)Managed internally by Route 53, no KMSSigns the zone's other records

Building the Chain of Trust

Once the keys exist, Route 53 does the rest of the signing automatically:

  1. The public parts of both the KSK and ZSK are added to a DNSKEY record in the hosted zone.
  2. The private KSK signs that DNSKEY record, producing an RRSIG record — proof the DNSKEY record itself is valid and unmodified.
  3. The parent zone (e.g. the .org TLD) needs a DS (Delegation Signer) record — a hash of the KSK's public part — to extend the chain of trust downward. If the domain is registered through Route 53, this can be added directly from the console/CLI; otherwise, it has to be added manually wherever the domain is registered.
Exam tip: where each piece lives
Three details are the ones most likely to be tested directly: the KMS key backing the KSK must be in us-east-1; the ZSK is Route-53-internal, no KMS; and the DS record lives in the parent zone (not your own zone) to link your zone's signature into the wider chain of trust.

Operational Notes

  • Enabling DNSSEC caps a hosted zone's TTLs at one week maximum, regardless of what you'd previously set.
  • Set up CloudWatch alarms for DNSSEC internal failure and key-signing key action needed — both indicate an urgent problem, either with the KSK itself or with the KMS interaction.
  • You can additionally enable DNSSEC validation for VPCs: for DNSSEC-enabled zones, any record that fails signature validation is withheld rather than returned. Non-DNSSEC zones are entirely unaffected by this setting.
  • Timing matters in production: AWS documents specific wait periods (tied to your existing TTLs) that must be respected between steps — rushing this on a live domain can cause a resolution outage.
