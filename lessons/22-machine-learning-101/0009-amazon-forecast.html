Amazon Forecast

Lesson 09 of 11 · ~5 min

Source: Adrian Cantrill SAA-C03 · What Is Amazon Forecast?

Service lifecycle: closed to new customers
As of July 29, 2024, AWS closed Amazon Forecast to new customers — it no longer accepts sign-ups from accounts that haven't already used it. Existing customers can keep using the service as normal, and AWS continues to maintain security, availability, and performance, but no new features are planned. For new forecasting projects, AWS points customers toward Amazon SageMaker (for example, with AutoGluon-TimeSeries or Chronos foundation models) instead. The exam may still test the underlying architecture and concepts covered below even though the product itself is winding down for new adopters.

Despite the name, Amazon Forecast has nothing to do with the weather — it's a managed service for time-series forecasting: predicting future values of something based on how it has behaved over time. Typical targets include retail demand, supply chain planning, staffing levels, energy requirements, server capacity, and web traffic.

Historical Data and Related Data

Forecast learns from two kinds of input:

Historical data
The core time series itself — for example, an item, a timestamp, and a quantity sold. This lets Forecast trend how something has behaved over time.
Related data
Optional contextual factors that plausibly influence the trend — things like promotions or weather. Weather in particular can swing demand in either direction: some conditions keep people home, others send them shopping, and even online demand can shift (heavy rain nudging demand for certain clothing, for instance).

Forecast combines both inputs to learn what "normal" looks like over a given period, then produces two outputs: a forecast — the predicted future trend — and forecast explainability, a breakdown of why the forecast moves the way it does, such as attributing a demand spike to a specific promotion or weather pattern.

Access and Positioning

Forecast can be used through the console — which includes visualization of past data, future predictions, and explainability — as well as through the CLI, APIs, and the Python SDK. It's a backend, fairly niche service: something consumed as part of a larger business process or application rather than used as a standalone destination.
