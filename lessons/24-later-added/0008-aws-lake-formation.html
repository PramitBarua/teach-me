AWS Lake Formation

Lesson 8 of 23 · ~6 min

Source: What is AWS Lake Formation?

A data lake is a pile of data in S3, described by tables in the Glue Data Catalog and queried with Athena, Redshift Spectrum, EMR or Quick. Securing that pile with S3 bucket policies alone becomes painful fast. Bucket policies understand objects and prefixes, but your analysts think in databases, tables and columns. AWS Lake Formation closes that gap. It centrally governs, secures and shares data in a data lake.

How It Works

Register S3 locations
You register S3 paths with Lake Formation. Its metadata lives in the Glue Data Catalog, and Glue crawlers can populate that catalog.
Database-style permissions
Lake Formation has its own permission model that works like a relational database: you grant and revoke SELECT, INSERT and so on, on databases and tables.
Fine-grained access
Permissions go down to the column, row and cell level, using data filters. For example, analysts in the EU see only EU rows and no ssn column.
LF-Tags (tag-based access control)
Attach tags such as classification=confidential to databases, tables or columns, then grant on tags instead of on hundreds of individual tables. This scales far better.
Cross-account sharing
Share catalog tables with other accounts or with the whole Organization, without copying any data.

These permissions are enforced by the integrated engines: Athena, Redshift Spectrum, EMR, Glue and Quick. Access is auditable through CloudTrail.

Spotting Lake Formation
"Data lake" together with "column-level or row-level access", "centrally manage permissions for Athena and Redshift Spectrum users", or "share data lake tables across accounts" points to Lake Formation. Bucket policies or IAM policies per prefix are the distractor that "works but doesn't scale". They also can't hide a single column inside a Parquet file.

Retrieval Practice

Answer from memory. Explanations appear after each answer.

1. Analysts query a customers table in an S3 data lake through Athena. Marketing analysts must not see the email and phone columns, but finance analysts must. What's the simplest way to enforce this?

2. A data lake has 900 tables, and new ones arrive weekly. Access rules depend on data sensitivity (public, internal, confidential). How should permissions be managed so they scale?

3. A central data account owns the data lake. Three business-unit accounts need to query certain tables with their own Athena, without copying data. Which approach fits?
