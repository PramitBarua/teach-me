EC2 Basics

EC2 Purchase Options (Part 1)

Lesson 16 of 21 · ~9 min

Source: Adrian Cantrill SAA-C03 · EC2 On-Demand Instances

EC2 instances are billed differently depending on the purchase option you choose at launch — AWS's official term for what many engineers still call "launch types." Every purchase option makes a different trade-off between price, commitment, and interruption risk. This lesson covers the two ends of that spectrum: On-Demand, which asks for no commitment at all, and Spot, which asks for none either but can take your capacity away at any moment. Part 2 picks up with Reserved Instances and the dedicated hardware options.

On-Demand: the default

On-Demand is deliberately unremarkable. Different customers' instances are packed onto the same shared pool of underlying EC2 hosts, which lets AWS allocate hardware efficiently — and that efficiency is why the On-Demand starting price is reasonable in the first place. You're billed per second while an instance is in the running state; stop it and the compute charge stops too, though attached resources like EBS volumes keep billing regardless of instance state because they consume capacity independent of whether the instance is running.

On-Demand has no interruptions — barring a genuine failure, an instance runs until you decide otherwise — but it also carries no capacity priority. If AWS suffers a major failure and remaining capacity is scarce, Reserved purchases are served first. On-Demand customers get whatever is left over.

Exam tip
Treat On-Demand as your default assumption for any exam scenario. Only move to a different purchase option once the question gives you a concrete reason to — a long-term commitment, a cost constraint, or a licensing requirement. See the EC2 On-Demand Instances guide for the full billing model.

On-Demand fits short-term workloads, workloads of unknown duration or type, and anything — short-term or not — that simply cannot tolerate interruption.

Spot: spare capacity at a discount

At any given moment, EC2 hosts running On-Demand and Reserved instances still have unused capacity. Spot is AWS selling that spare capacity at a steep discount. Within each region, for each instance type, AWS tracks available spare capacity and publishes a spot price for it. You set a maximum price you're willing to pay, but you only ever pay the current spot price — never more, even if your maximum is higher.

If the spot price rises above your maximum — because AWS needs the capacity back for On-Demand demand, for example — your Spot instances are interrupted. AWS reclaims Spot capacity with only a very short interruption notice, so Spot should never be treated as reliable, continuously-available compute.

AttributeSpot Instances
DiscountUp to ~90% versus On-Demand
Price mechanicYou pay the current spot price, capped by your maximum bid
ReliabilityCan be reclaimed whenever the spot price exceeds your maximum
Best forFault-tolerant, flexible, interruption-tolerant workloads

Good fits: highly parallel batch or scientific workloads that can be split into many pieces and rerun on failure, bursty jobs like media or image processing, cost-sensitive workloads that wouldn't be economical On-Demand, and stateless tiers where session data isn't stored on the instance. See the EC2 Spot Instances guide for the full mechanics.

Exam tip
Never pick Spot for anything that can't tolerate interruption — domain controllers, mail servers, traditional always-on websites, or anything business-critical. If a scenario mentions "cannot tolerate disruption," Spot is the wrong answer regardless of how attractive the discount sounds.
  • On-Demand — no commitment, no discount, no interruption, no capacity priority.
  • Spot — no commitment, deepest discount, can be interrupted at any time, no capacity priority.
