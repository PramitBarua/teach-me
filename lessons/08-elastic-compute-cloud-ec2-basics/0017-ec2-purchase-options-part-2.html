EC2 Purchase Options (Part 2)

Lesson 17 of 21 · ~11 min

Source: Adrian Cantrill SAA-C03 · EC2 Reserved Instances · EC2 Dedicated Hosts

Part 1 covered On-Demand and Spot — the two purchase options that ask for no commitment. This lesson covers the options built around commitment: Reserved Instances, which commit you to long-term usage in exchange for a discount, and the two hardware-isolation options, Dedicated Hosts and Dedicated Instances.

Reserved Instances (Standard)

A Reserved Instance (RI) is a commitment you make to AWS to consume EC2 resources over a fixed term, in return for a reduced — or in some payment structures, eliminated — per-second price. Reservations attach to a specific instance type and can be scoped to a single Availability Zone or to an entire region. A reservation is a billing construct: it doesn't change how an instance runs, only what you pay for a matching instance. If a running instance matches an active reservation, the discount applies automatically; if the reservation goes unmatched, you still pay for it — the commitment is what you're billed for, not the usage.

Exam tip
An unused reservation is not refunded or paused — you're billed for the commitment regardless of consumption. Only reserve capacity you're confident you'll actually run, and reserve it for the steady, unchanging parts of your architecture.

Reservations can also apply partially. A reservation sized for a t3.large applied against a larger running instance in the same family still contributes a partial discount toward that larger instance's cost.

Term and payment options

You commit for either a one-year or three-year term — three years unlocks a bigger discount but carries more risk if your requirements change. Independently, you choose how much of that commitment to pay upfront:

Payment optionHow it worksDiscount
No UpfrontReduced per-second fee for the whole term, billed whether the instance runs or notSmallest
Partial UpfrontA smaller lump sum paid upfront, plus a lower per-second feeMiddle ground
All UpfrontThe entire term paid at purchase; no further per-second feeLargest

The single largest discount available across all Reserved options is a three-year, All Upfront commitment. See the EC2 Reserved Instances guide for current discount ranges — expect roughly a ~40–70% saving versus On-Demand, depending on term, payment option, and instance family.

Reserved Instances suit infrastructure with known, long-term, consistent usage that requires the lowest possible cost and cannot tolerate interruption — the opposite profile from Spot.

Dedicated Hosts

A Dedicated Host is a physical EC2 server allocated to you in its entirety, sized for a specific instance family (for example, all-A, all-C, or all-R instances). You pay for the host itself — instances launched onto it carry no separate per-second charge — and you can launch a mix of sizes up to the host's full physical capacity. Because it's your host and only your host, you get visibility into its physical sockets and cores, and a feature called host affinity that keeps a stopped-and-started instance pinned to the same physical host.

The reason to reach for Dedicated Hosts is almost always licensing — some software is still licensed per physical socket or per physical core, and that licensing model needs visibility into physical hardware that a normal virtualized instance can't provide.

Dedicated Instances

Dedicated Instances are a middle ground: your instances run on hardware used only by you, so AWS guarantees no other customer's instances share that physical host — but you don't manage or pay for the host itself, and you get no visibility into sockets or cores. You pay a small hourly fee per region where you use them, plus the per-instance cost. Choose Dedicated Instances when a compliance or contractual requirement says "don't share physical hardware," without needing the licensing-driven control that Dedicated Hosts provide.

Exam tip
If a question mentions bring-your-own-license tied to physical cores or sockets, the answer is Dedicated Hosts. If it only says "must not share underlying hardware" with no licensing angle, the answer is Dedicated Instances. See EC2 Dedicated Hosts for the full comparison AWS publishes against Dedicated Instances.

For the exam, focus your effort on On-Demand, Reserved, and Spot — they appear far more often than the dedicated hardware options.
