Direct Connect (DX): Concepts

Lesson 04 of 16 · ~9 min

Source: Adrian Cantrill SAA-C03 · What is Direct Connect? · Requesting cross connects at Direct Connect locations

Direct Connect (DX) is a dedicated physical network connection into an AWS Region. Ordering a Direct Connect from AWS gets you a port allocation at a DX location — nothing more. AWS doesn't run a cable to your building; it's on you to physically connect to that port, either yourself or through a comms partner.

Three Physical Locations

Think of a Direct Connect architecture as three distinct physical places:

  1. Your business premises — with a customer premises router.
  2. A DX location — a facility housing DX infrastructure.
  3. An AWS Region — the destination, connected to its nearby DX location(s) by AWS's own resilient, high-speed links.
Exam tip — flagged in the course diagram: DX locations are not AWS-owned
A DX location is not an AWS-owned building. It's typically a large, third-party-operated regional data center in which AWS leases space — the AWS cage — to house its own DX routers. Your organization (or your comms partner) separately leases its own space in the same facility. This distinction — AWS is a tenant at the DX location, not the owner — is easy to assume wrong and worth remembering precisely as stated.

Cages and Cross Connects

Inside the DX location:

  • The AWS cage holds the AWS DX routers — the service endpoints.
  • If you're a large organization with your own footprint at the location, you rent a customer cage housing your own DX-capable router.
  • If you don't have (or want) your own space there, a communications/network partner rents a comms partner cage instead.
Exam tip — flagged in the course diagram: comms partners extend the connection for you
If you don't have your own equipment or cage at the DX location, a communications/network partner can extend the connection from the DX location into your business premises on your behalf, using their own presence there. You don't need a physical footprint at the DX location yourself to use Direct Connect — the comms-partner-cage pattern exists precisely to cover that gap.

Whichever cage your router sits in, connecting it to the AWS DX router requires ordering a cross connect — the physical fiber cable within the DX location linking your port (or your partner's port) to the AWS DX port. If a comms partner is involved, they're also responsible for extending that connectivity onward to your actual premises.

Port Speeds and Billing

The transcript's course-era speed options were 1 Gbps, 10 Gbps, or 100 Gbps. AWS has since added a 400 Gbps dedicated connection option at select locations, so current published speeds for dedicated connections span 1/10/100/400 Gbps (hosted connections, provisioned through a DX partner, support additional finer-grained speeds below 1 Gbps too). Billing has two components: an hourly port charge (based on location and speed) plus a charge for outbound data transfer — inbound data transfer is free.

Provisioning time and resilience — know these cold
Direct Connect is slow to provision — AWS needs time to allocate the port, and if the physical cabling from the DX location to your premises doesn't already exist, laying it can take weeks to months. And because it's ultimately one physical cable, DX has no built-in resilience: cut the cable and the connection is down. Any redundancy has to be deliberately designed in with multiple, physically separate Direct Connects — a topic covered in the next lesson.

Why Choose Direct Connect

Because it's a dedicated physical link that bypasses the public internet, DX offers low and consistent latency and the highest achievable throughput for hybrid connectivity — a dedicated port largely guarantees you can hit close to its rated speed, unlike an IPSec VPN, which adds encryption overhead and transits shared internet paths.

Virtual Interfaces (VIFs)

A single physical DX connection is logically divided using virtual interfaces. There are three types, at a conceptual level for now:

VIF typePurpose
Private VIFConnects to a virtual private gateway attached to a VPC — access to private AWS resources
Public VIFReaches AWS public-zone services (e.g. S3, SQS) using their public IP ranges
Transit VIFConnects to a Transit Gateway — covered in depth in a later lesson
DX cannot reach the public internet
Direct Connect gives you paths to AWS private services (via a private VIF) and AWS public-zone services (via a public VIF) — but never the general public internet. If you need internet access over a DX-connected network, you have to add your own proxy or other networking appliance to provide it; DX itself simply doesn't do that.
