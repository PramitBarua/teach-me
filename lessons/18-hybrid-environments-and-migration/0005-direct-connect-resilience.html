Direct Connect (DX): Resilience

Lesson 05 of 16 · ~8 min

Source: Adrian Cantrill SAA-C03 · Resilience in AWS Direct Connect

A typical Direct Connect deployment has several physical components: your AWS Region (always assumed highly available), a DX location (a data center housing AWS DX routers), your customer (or provider) DX router at that same location, a cross-connect cable joining the two routers, an extension cable carrying the connection back to your customer premises, and a customer premises router at the far end.

DX is not resilient by default — it's just physical hardware
A single, simply-ordered Direct Connect has seven single points of failure: the DX location, the AWS DX router, the cross-connect cable, the customer DX router, the extension cable, the customer premises, and the customer premises router. Every one of these is a physical thing that can fail in isolation. Direct Connect is a flexible product that can be made highly resilient — but only if you deliberately architect it that way.

Step 1: Two DX Ports, One Location, One Premises

The first improvement is ordering two DX connections into the same DX location. AWS provisions these onto separate DX routers, and you cross-connect each into a separate customer DX router, extended to two customer premises routers at a single customer premises.

This tolerates a failure of either DX router, cross-connect, or extension — but two major single points of failure remain: the DX location itself (if it fails, both paths are lost) and the single customer premises (same problem on the other end).

The hidden single point of failure: shared cable routes
Even with two extensions running between the DX location and your premises, both physical cables often follow the same duct or trench — the same sidewalk, the same conduit. Roadworks or a single dig can sever both "independent" connections at once. Ordering two connections doesn't guarantee physically diverse paths; you have to verify the routing.

Step 2: Two DX Locations, Two Customer Premises

The next evolution uses two entirely different DX locations, each with one DX port cross-connected to one customer DX router, extended to two separate customer premises — ideally geographically spread out.

This tolerates the failure of an entire DX location on the AWS side, or an entire premises on the customer side, because each path now has independent start and end points. The only remaining outage scenario is a full location failure combined with a hardware failure in the one remaining path.

Step 3: Maximum Resilience

The gold-standard architecture combines both ideas: two DX locations, and at each location, two DX ports on separate equipment with two customer DX routers, all extended to two customer premises, each with dual customer routers.

This tolerates a full DX location failure and a hardware failure in the surviving location's remaining pair, because the extensions between two different physical buildings on each side naturally follow separate routes. There is no single component anywhere in the path whose failure takes down connectivity.

Exam tip: the AWS Direct Connect Resiliency Toolkit mirrors this progression
AWS's own connection wizard, the Direct Connect Resiliency Toolkit, formalizes this build-up into named models — from development/test (no SLA) up through high resiliency and maximum resiliency (two locations, multiple connections each). If a question describes a resilience requirement, map it back to how many locations and how many ports per location are involved; that's the pattern being tested. A Site-to-Site VPN can also serve as a backup transit path for Direct Connect, covered elsewhere.

Retrieval Practice

Answer from memory. Explanations appear after each answer.

1. An architecture uses two DX ports at a single DX location, cross-connected to two customer DX routers, both extended into a single customer premises. What is the biggest remaining risk?

2. A company orders two Direct Connect extensions between one DX location and its single office, expecting full redundancy. Both connections fail at once during nearby roadworks. What most likely explains this?

3. Which architecture achieves Direct Connect's maximum level of resilience, tolerating both a full DX location failure and a subsequent hardware failure in the surviving location?
